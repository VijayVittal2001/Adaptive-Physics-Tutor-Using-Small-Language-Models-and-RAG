Simple Theory

An intrinsic semiconductor is a pure semiconductor without any impurity. Silicon and Germanium are common examples.

In a pure silicon crystal, each silicon atom forms covalent bonds with four neighbouring silicon atoms. At low temperature, these bonds are intact and there are almost no free charge carriers.

When temperature increases, thermal energy breaks some covalent bonds. When a bond breaks, one electron becomes free and leaves an empty place behind. This empty place is called a hole.

In an intrinsic semiconductor, free electrons and holes are created in pairs. Therefore, the number of electrons is equal to the number of holes.

Board Exam Answer

An intrinsic semiconductor is a pure semiconductor in which no impurity is added. Silicon and Germanium are examples.

In its crystal structure, every Si or Ge atom forms covalent bonds with four neighbouring atoms. At low temperature, all covalent bonds are intact. At room temperature, thermal energy breaks some covalent bonds and produces free electrons and holes. The vacancy left by an escaped electron is called a hole and behaves like a positive charge carrier.

In intrinsic semiconductors, the number of free electrons is equal to the number of holes:

ne = nh = ni

The total current is due to both electron current and hole current:

I = Ie + Ih
