Simple Theory

An extrinsic semiconductor is formed by adding a very small amount of suitable impurity to a pure semiconductor such as Silicon or Germanium.

This process is called doping, and the impurity atoms are called dopants. Doping increases the number of charge carriers and therefore increases conductivity.

There are two types:

  • n-type semiconductor: formed by adding pentavalent impurity such as Phosphorus, Arsenic or Antimony. Electrons are majority carriers.
  • p-type semiconductor: formed by adding trivalent impurity such as Boron, Aluminium or Indium. Holes are majority carriers.

Board Exam Answer

The conductivity of an intrinsic semiconductor is low at room temperature. To increase its conductivity, a small amount of suitable impurity is added to the pure semiconductor. This process is called doping, and the impurity atoms are called dopants. The semiconductor formed after doping is called an extrinsic semiconductor.

If a pentavalent impurity such as P, As or Sb is added to Si or Ge, four electrons form covalent bonds and the fifth electron becomes free. This gives an n-type semiconductor, where electrons are majority carriers and holes are minority carriers.

If a trivalent impurity such as B, Al or In is added to Si or Ge, one covalent bond remains incomplete and a hole is formed. This gives a p-type semiconductor, where holes are majority carriers and electrons are minority carriers.
